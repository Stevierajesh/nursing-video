Story: 


51 year old Timmy Buttons has been wheeled into the ER after complaining of persistent swelling in his feet and legs and cramping. From his original vitals, the nurse, Sally Osborn, does not see much that triggers her. But from her experience, she knows that this symptom of persistent swelling of the feet is consistent with circulatory decline so she decides to use Salus to complete her risk assessment. She clicks that she is very worried about the patient and states the reason as “In my experience, I have seen that swelling of the feet while cramping is a symptom of circulatory decline”. She submits this risk assessment and Salus gives a risk score of 3/10. Salus sees that the vitals currently represent almost normal trends but it took account of the risk assessment of the nurse to yield the risk score of 3/10. Salus now will keep tracking the real time vital trends of Timmy and if they change for the better or worse, there will be a notification sent to the nurse and the doctor about the change in the risk score. After around 10 minutes, Timmy’s vitals now show elevated levels of HR, RR, and SPO2 decreases. This change in vitals sends an alert to the physician and Sally Osborn who then comes and attends to Timmy Buttons. Salus understands that sometimes physicians aren't always able to attend every patient on time so it repaged the physician every 2 minutes till they came to the care of Timmy Buttons. She then records another risk assessment that marks that she is very worried about circulatory decline. Salus recognizes this and increases the risk score to a 6/10 now changing Timmy Button’s status from green to yellow. Another 20 minutes later, these trends are consistent and Salus sends out an urgent alarm to both the physician and Sally Osborn to come to room 609 to attend to the patient. The flashing red warning on the unit map made it abundantly clear for both Sally and the physician to come to room 609. From here, the physician was able to assess the damage and use Salus to send a prescription straight to the pharmacy for immediate retrieval. After Timmy is administered aid, his vitals normalize and he is able to be saved due to the input of the nurse, the work by the physician, and the attentiveness that Salus provided. 



Reason why we made SALUS and description of app: 

At 4 a.m., a nurse noticed a patient admitted for a kidney stone was confused. Instead of blaming the pain medication, she raised the alarm. It was septic shock, and the patient recovered fully. The nurse’s gut feeling recognised even before any vitals did, which saved her life.
That instinct is usually right: about 77 percent of the time, when a nurse is worried enough to call for help. But there is no product or app that records it. If she hadn’t spoken up, no system would have noticed.
Project SALUS makes that instinct count. Nurses rate their level of concern for a patient on a scale of 1 to 10, and SALUS compares that rating against the patient’s live vitals. When both the nurse’s concern and the vitals based risk score exceed 8, the responsible physician is paged immediately, and re-paged every two minutes until the alert is acknowledged.
SALUS combines continuous vitals monitoring with a unit map of the hospital. When a patient’s vitals have a concerning fluctuation, that bed flashes red on the map and the assigned nurse receives an instant pop up alert. This ensures that there’s prompt care provided. SALUS generates recommended medications, and nurses can accept them or propose alternatives. The physician then reviews eight sections of the patient’s history in a single view and approves or modifies the medications.
SALUS addresses several challenges in critical care. It captures nursing intuition, a valuable early warning sign that no standard system currently uses. It reduces alarm fatigue by requiring agreement between human judgment and objective data. And it eliminates delays and fragmentation by escalating concerns quickly
